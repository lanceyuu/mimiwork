# Evaluating a Theoretical Contribution + Contribution-Statement Templates

Sources: Whetten (1989), AMR 14(4): 490–495; Sutton & Staw (1995), ASQ 40(3): 371–384; Corley &
Gioia (2011), AMR 36: 12–32; Campbell (1990); the SIOP 2015 Theory Debate (Cucina, Cortina,
Mathieu, McDaniel, Ashkanasy).

Use this to pressure-test a contribution *before reviewers do*, and to draft the explicit
contribution statements that reviewers look for.

## 1. Whetten's seven reviewer questions (roughly in order of how often they're invoked)

1. **What's new?** Does the paper make a significant, value-added contribution to current
   thinking? (Not necessarily a brand-new theory — but modifications must alter existing views in
   important ways.)
2. **So what?** Will it change the practice of research/theory in this area? Are links to research
   evident?
3. **Why so?** Are the underlying logic and supporting evidence compelling? Are assumptions
   explicit and believable?
4. **Well done?** Does it reflect seasoned, thorough thinking? Are *multiple* theoretical elements
   covered (What / How / Why / When-Where-Who) rather than one in isolation?
5. **Done well?** Is it well written, logically flowing, the right length, professional?
6. **Why now?** Is the topic timely — does it advance, stimulate, or revitalize a current
   conversation?
7. **Who cares?** How large is the audience? Even specialized papers should link to core concepts
   of the field.

> Whetten's three themes for critique-based contributions: (a) address **multiple** elements of a
> theory, not one; (b) marshal **compelling evidence** — logical, empirical, or epistemological;
> (c) propose **remedies/alternatives**, don't just point out flaws. A critic shares responsibility
> for the improved conceptualization.

## 2. Two dimensions every contribution is judged on

- **Scope vs Degree** (Whetten). *Scope* = how much of the field is affected (general vs
  middle-range). *Degree* = how radically different the proposal is from current thinking. **Degree
  matters more than scope** for the merit of a contribution. A modest-scope but high-degree idea
  beats a broad-but-obvious one.
- **Originality vs Utility / Scientific vs Practical** (Corley & Gioia). Originality can be
  *incremental* (advancing within an accepted frame) or *revelatory* (revealing something we'd not
  otherwise have seen). Top theory outlets have shifted toward **revelatory, non-obvious**
  contributions. Utility can be *scientific* (helps research) or *practical* (helps practice). A
  strong contribution scores on both axes; "originality stands out as the dimension de rigueur."

## 3. The strong-theory norm — and the counter-position

**The permissive norm (Sutton & Staw, 1995):** when a theory is genuinely *interesting/important*,
"the standards used to evaluate how well it is tested or grounded need to be relaxed, not
strengthened… major contributions can be made when data are more illustrative than definitive."
A small set of interviews, a demonstration experiment, a pilot, or some archival data may be all
that's needed to show why a process *might* be true. Translate for users: **a bold, well-reasoned
idea earns latitude on data — but never on logic.**

**The counter-position (SIOP debate — use as a caution):**
- **Theory is not the dependent variable** (Campbell). It is a valuable *process*, not a desired
  *end-state*. "The most elegant formalisms cannot substitute for better ideas… better theories
  are simply better substantive ideas about what things mean, how things work, or what the serious
  problems are." Don't fetishize the *form* of theory over substance.
- **The "interesting" trap** (Pillutla & Thau): demanding that results be counterintuitive can
  fragment a field and incentivize chasing surprising "facts" over cumulative knowledge.
- **HARKing & post-hoc theory** (McDaniel; O'Boyle et al.): presenting after-the-fact explanations
  as a priori hypotheses inflates Type-I error and corrodes trust. A theory built to fit results
  already analyzed is a *story*, not a test.
- **Replication caveat** (Cortina, McDaniel): a default scientific posture is that *all theories
  are at least somewhat wrong*; a contribution can be making a theory *less wrong* or mapping its
  boundary conditions — which the publication system under-rewards.

**How to hold both:** push for a bold, interesting, well-reasoned contribution (Sutton & Staw,
Davis) **and** be honest about logic, assumptions, and boundary conditions, and don't dress up a
post-hoc story as confirmation (the SIOP cautions). Tell users which side of this tension their
paper is on.

## 4. Contribution-statement templates

Reviewers want the contribution stated *explicitly*. Offer the user one of these scaffolds, filled
from their material. Always pair the claim with the **mechanism (Why)** and the **boundary
(When/Where/Who)**.

**A. The problematization → resolution statement (strongest default):**
> "Research on [topic] has largely assumed [taken-for-granted assumption X]. Drawing on [conceptual
> resource / reasoning move], we show that [not-X] under [conditions]. This re-specifies [the
> mechanism / the How and Why], and implies [consequence for theory and practice]."

**B. The new-construct statement (heuristic analogy):**
> "We introduce [construct], conceptualized as [definition], to capture [aspect of the phenomenon]
> that existing constructs ([A], [B]) miss. [Construct] matters because [mechanism], which predicts
> [novel relationship] beyond [established account]."

**C. The mechanism / qualifying statement (causal analogy or lab-rat counterfactual):**
> "While prior work establishes that [A relates to B], the *why* remains underspecified. We
> theorize [mediator/moderator M] as the mechanism, by analogy to [source domain] / by contrasting
> [rival causal models]. This explains [previously anomalous finding] and bounds the effect to
> [conditions]."

**D. The integrative / lateral-move statement:**
> "Two streams — [model 1] and [model 2] — have explained [phenomenon] in parallel with little
> dialogue. By comparing their causal logics, we develop an integrative account in which [synthesis],
> resolving [contradiction] and offering greater [scope / predictive ability]."

**E. The perspective / re-signification statement (perspectival theorizing):**
> "We re-read [topic] not as [default framing] but as [re-signified framing]. This deeper reading
> reveals [insight invisible under the default] and redirects inquiry toward [new questions]."

For each, generate a one-paragraph version for the introduction and a one-sentence version for the
abstract. Provide the **"moves I made"** note: which reasoning mode, which problematization type,
which building block strengthened.

## 5. Self-review red flags (run before submission)

- The "theory section" is mostly **references + a variable list + a figure + hypotheses** → the
  *Why* is missing (Sutton & Staw). Fix before anything else.
- A **long list of hypotheses** with no single organizing logic ("buckshot") → reviewers read it as
  hypotheses-in-lieu-of-theory.
- The contribution is **"we added variable X"** → weakest move; show how X changes the How and Why.
- The contribution is stated only as a **gap** ("X has not been studied") → upgrade from
  incompleteness to inadequacy/incommensurability, or to a problematized assumption.
- The figure has **arrows the text never explains** → spell out the logic behind each arrow.
- No **boundary conditions** → the claim floats free of context; add When/Where/Who.
- The "interesting" claim is **counterintuitive but unmotivated** → risk of HARKing; ground it or
  soften it.
- You cannot fill in **"past work assumes X; we show not-X"** → you have a finding, not yet a
  contribution.
