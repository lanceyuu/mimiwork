# Modes of Reasoning that Produce New Theory

Source: Cornelissen & Durand (2014), *Moving Forward: Developing Theoretical Contributions in
Management Studies*, Journal of Management Studies 51(6): 995–1022. (Builds on Weick, 1989;
Gentner; Goodman, 1947; Turner, 1996; Tsang & Elsaesser, 2011.)

**Core claim:** original theory does not appear by magic or by "filling gaps." It is produced by
two families of disciplined cognitive reasoning — **analogical** (import structure from another
domain) and **counterfactual** (challenge what is taken for granted). "It is literally only
through analogical and counterfactual reasoning that we can move forward." Use this file to find
the *specific* move that fits a user's material.

## 1. The six modes at a glance

| Family | Mode | What it does | Typical use |
|--------|------|--------------|-------------|
| Analogy | **Heuristic analogy** | A thought-propelling metaphor that seeds new constructs/assumptions, then is discarded | **Theory building** — introduce or re-conceptualize a construct |
| Analogy | **Causal analogy** | Transfer a *causal schema* from a related domain to model mechanisms | **Qualifying theory** — elaborate/specify causal relationships |
| Analogy | **Constitutive analogy** | Import a *whole framework* (vocabulary, assumptions, causal structure) and blend it in | **Theory expansion** — a radically new perspective/vocabulary |
| Counterfactual | **Spotlight counterfactual** | Ask "what if the default assumption is wrong?" to provoke reflection | **Prod / problematize** — challenge default assumptions (starting point only) |
| Counterfactual | **Lab-rat counterfactual** | Contrast the actual causal model with a deliberately altered one, all else held constant | **Qualifying theory** — isolate/sharpen causal factors |
| Counterfactual | **Constitutive counterfactual** | Replace the entire received frame with a new conceptual organization | **Theory building (frame-breaking)** — recast received thought |

The six are conceptually distinct but **not mutually exclusive** — strong programs shift between
them and combine them.

## 2. Analogical reasoning

Analogy = seeing a target domain (the thing you want to explain) "in terms of" a source domain
you understand. Distance matters: *near* analogies (from adjacent fields) refine; *distant*
analogies (biology→organizations, computation→mind) provoke "surprisingly new and revelatory
insights" — provided the comparison is apt.

- **Heuristic analogy** — "thought-propelling," used episodically then "kicked away once the new
  theoretical plateau has been reached" (Brown). It introduces a new construct or
  re-conceptualizes one; the spark often disappears from the final theory.
  - *Examples:* organizations as a "nexus of contracts" (Jensen & Meckling); **sensemaking**
    (Weick) grew from analogies to cognitive dissonance + biological enaction; psychological
    contract; escalation of commitment; expectancy theory; upper echelons.
- **Causal analogy** — abstracts a *causal template* and transfers the *relational/causal
  structure* (not just surface similarity) from source to target. "An assertion that a relational
  structure that normally applies in one domain can be applied in another" (Gentner).
  - *Examples:* **population ecology** (Hannan & Freeman) modelled organizations on the
    growth/survival dynamics of biological populations; resource-based theory; organizational
    effectiveness; job characteristics theory.
- **Constitutive analogy** — a "full" analogy that imports *large parts* of the source domain —
  vocabulary, base assumptions, causal structure — and **blends** the two frames into an emergent
  whole (conceptual integration; Fauconnier & Turner). Over time the analogy becomes taken as
  *literally true*.
  - *Examples:* **mind-as-computation** → behavioural theory of the firm; the computational image
    of cognition. Such analogies, when successful, "become the property of the entire scientific
    community" (Boyd).

## 3. Counterfactual reasoning

Counterfactual = imagining alternatives to existing assumptions/constructs/causal models through
contrastive "what if?" questioning — to modify or challenge the received base.

- **Spotlight counterfactual** — "what if we think differently about the base assumptions?" It
  shines a light on taken-for-granted assumptions to stimulate reflexivity. **This is
  problematization** (Alvesson & Sandberg) — e.g. conceiving leadership as situationally emergent
  rather than trait-determined. *Crucially, it is a starting point, not yet a contribution:* it
  "stops short of specifying antecedents, consequents, and principles of causal connection." To
  contribute, it must connect to new constructs or real revision. Don't let a paper stop here.
- **Lab-rat counterfactual** — contrast the actual scenario with a "reasonably different imagined
  scenario of causal patterns," holding everything else constant (as in a controlled lab
  manipulation), to isolate which factors carry the causal work and rule others out (Mackie's
  "causal field"; Pearl). Sharpens and defines causality.
  - *Examples:* **transaction cost economics** (Williamson); the Durand & Vaara approach to
    competitive advantage; social cognitive theory; referent cognitions theory.
- **Constitutive counterfactual** — the most powerful: through counterfactual reframing, the
  *entire* prior frame is "wholly supplanted by a new conceptual frame" with different guiding
  assumptions and causal explanations (Goodman, 1947; Fauconnier & Turner). A complex *blending*
  of proposed and prior frames.
  - *Examples:* **prospect theory** (Kahneman & Tversky) reframed decisions around a reference
    point (gains/losses) rather than wealth-maximization, importing psychological assumptions
    (framing, heuristics); **stakeholder theory** (managers attend to stakeholders, not industry);
    resource dependence; institutional theory; evolutionary economics. Corley & Gioia (2011): the
    most-cited AMR contributions came "not so much from introducing new constructs but… by
    offering a novel approach to integrating prior thought… that constituted a different way of
    understanding some phenomenon."

## 4. Which mode for which goal

Diagnose the user's intent, then point to the mode:

- **"I want to introduce/rename a new construct."** → Heuristic analogy. Find a vivid source
  domain; let it seed the construct; plan to drop the metaphor later.
- **"I need to specify the mechanism / why A causes B."** → Causal analogy *or* lab-rat
  counterfactual. Borrow a causal schema, or contrast rival causal models to isolate the path.
- **"My field's whole framing feels wrong / I want a new perspective."** → Constitutive analogy
  (import a new frame) or constitutive counterfactual (replace the old frame). This is the
  frame-breaking, highest-impact territory.
- **"I want to challenge an assumption / problematize."** → Spotlight counterfactual — but warn
  them it must be *followed through* into constructs or revisions to count as a contribution.

Empirical pattern from the 24 landmark theories (Smith & Hitt, *Great Minds in Management*):
heuristic & causal reasoning dominate at the **micro** level (constructs, mechanisms);
**constitutive counterfactuals** dominate at the **macro** level (10 of 24 theories — they
emerged from critiques of neo-classical economics / rational agency). Striking absence of
*spotlight* counterfactuals among landmark theories — consistent with spotlights being only a
*starting point*.

## 5. Pathways — combining modes over a research program

- **Scaling up (vertical).** Start with a *heuristic analogy* (new construct) → add a *causal
  analogy* (specify relationships & mechanisms) → ground/refine empirically → an integrated, full
  theory. This is how a metaphor (e.g. sensemaking, upper echelons) grows into a broad
  perspective. The lesson for a user: *don't stop at the metaphor — push down into mechanisms.*
- **Lateral moves (horizontal).** Take two rival *causal models* of the **same** phenomenon
  (often running in parallel for decades) and analogically compare/contrast them to build an
  integrated model of greater scope and predictive ability — and to surface each model's
  underlying assumptions. (E.g. integrating "positive" and "critical" HRM models; corporate
  governance streams.) The lesson: *real progress often comes from synthesizing rivals, not from
  ever-narrower elaboration of one.*

> The broad takeaway to give users: novelty for its own sake is not the goal. The goal is to
> deploy a *specific* reasoning move — and often to **shift between or combine** moves — to
> advance the explanatory base. Name the move you (or they) are making; it disciplines the work
> and gives reviewers a recognizable logic.

## 6. Using this when coaching or drafting

- **Coaching:** ask "what is the source domain you're borrowing from?" and "what assumption are
  you challenging?" — those two questions locate the user in the analogy/counterfactual space.
  Then name the mode and its typical pitfalls.
- **Drafting:** when you write a framework, *explicitly* build it on a named move ("we draw a
  *causal analogy* from X to specify the mechanism by which…") and, in the "moves I made" note,
  say which mode and why. Reviewers reward a legible theoretical logic.
