## 1. Working definitions of theory

There is more agreement on what theory is *not* than on what it *is*, but the usable core
across the literature is:

- **Theory answers "why."** "Theory is the answer to queries of *why*. Theory is about the
  connections among phenomena, a story about why acts, events, structure, and thoughts occur"
  (Sutton & Staw, 1995, p. 378). Data describe *which* patterns were observed; theory explains
  *why* they were observed or are expected (Kaplan, 1964).
- **Theory is an explanatory system of causal relations.** "An analytic structure or system of
  causal associations that attempts to account for, explain, and predict empirical phenomena"
  (Durand & Vaara; Sutton & Staw).
- **Theory is a linguistic device to organize a complex empirical world.** "A statement of
  relations among concepts within a set of boundary assumptions and constraints… no more than a
  linguistic device used to organize a complex empirical world" (Bacharach, 1989, p. 496).
- **A good theory explains, predicts, and delights** (Weick, 1995). Strong theory "delves into
  underlying processes to understand the systematic reasons for a particular occurrence or
  nonoccurrence" (Sutton & Staw, 1995).

The word "theory" is an *umbrella/container term* (Merton, 1967): its referents range from
minor working hypotheses to grand axiomatic systems, which is why it "threatens to become
meaningless." Do not get stuck defining it — focus on the *function*: a compelling, logically
interconnected account of **why**.

## 2. The five impostors — what theory is NOT (Sutton & Staw, 1995)

Each is a legitimate, often necessary part of a paper. None of them, *by itself*, constitutes
theory. When a "theory section" is built mostly from these, that is the diagnosis of "weak
theory."

| # | Impostor | Why it isn't theory | The fix |
|---|----------|---------------------|---------|
| 1 | **References** | Listing prior theories and naming them ("psycho-social theory," "identity theory") signals membership, not logic. A "smoke screen" of citations can hide the absence of theory. | Explicate *which* concepts/causal arguments you borrow and *how* they link to your argument. |
| 2 | **Data & empirical findings** | Beta weights, factor loadings, informant quotes describe *what* was observed. "The data do not generate theory — only researchers do that" (Mintzberg). Brute empiricism = hypotheses motivated by prior data, not logic. | Supply the causal reasoning for *why* the pattern occurs/is expected. |
| 3 | **Lists of variables / constructs** | A construct dictionary, however well-defined, is "concepts and their definitions but no sentences" (Homans). Dividing the world into (e.g.) "personality vs situation" is not a theory of behavior. | Explain *why* the constructs come about and *why* they are connected. |
| 4 | **Diagrams & figures** | Boxes and arrows show *that* things connect but the arrow does not say *why*. Figures are "stage props, not the performance." | Spell out, in sentences, the logical nuances behind each causal arrow. Good theory is representational *and* verbal. |
| 5 | **Hypotheses / propositions** | Hypotheses are "concise statements about *what* is expected to occur, not *why*" (Sutton & Staw). A list of predictions — especially a "buckshot" spread of many — is not an explanation. | Precede predictions with the logical argument that produces them. |

Two telltale signs a paper has substituted hypotheses for theory: (a) so many hypotheses that
none is adequately motivated; (b) an introduction that ends in a long list of predictions or a
summarizing figure only loosely tied to the logic. Strong theory usually starts from one or two
ideas and builds a logically detailed case — *simplicity plus interconnectedness*.

## 3. The four building blocks (Whetten, 1989)

A complete theoretical argument has four elements. Use them as a checklist to locate what a
manuscript is missing.

- **What** — Which factors (constructs, variables) belong in the explanation?
  - Judged by two competing virtues: **comprehensiveness** (are all relevant factors included?)
    and **parsimony** (should some be dropped as low-value?). "Sensitivity to the competing
    virtues of parsimony and comprehensiveness is the hallmark of a good theorist." When mapping
    a topic, err toward inclusion early; it is easier to prune later — but this is not license
    for a kitchen-sink model.
- **How** — How are the factors related?
  - The "boxes and arrows." Adds order and introduces **causality**. Restrictions in *method*
    (inability to test a link) do not invalidate the *causal nature* of theory.
- **Why** — What are the underlying dynamics that justify the selection of factors and the
  proposed relationships?
  - **This is the theory.** The "theoretical glue that welds the model together." The answer to
    "*why should colleagues give credence to this representation?*" lies in the soundness of the
    underlying view of human nature, organizational requisites, or social process.
  - **What and How *describe*; only Why *explains*.** Most "weak theory" verdicts = a missing or
    shallow Why.
- **Who / Where / When** — The boundary conditions (range / temporal & contextual limits).
  - Set the generalizability of the claim. Especially important for theories grounded in
    experience, where "meaning is derived from context."

### How the blocks generate a contribution

- Adding/subtracting a **What** (a new variable) is the **weakest** move — "additions or
  deletions are seldom of sufficient magnitude to substantially alter the core logic." Reviewers
  rarely accept "I added variable X" as a contribution unless it changes the **How** and **Why**.
- The most fruitful — and hardest — avenue is changing the **Why**: borrowing a perspective from
  another field that alters the metaphors/gestalts underlying accepted theory, forcing a broad
  reconceptualization. (This connects to *constitutive* analogy/counterfactual reasoning — see
  `reasoning-modes.md`.)
- **Propositions vs hypotheses**: propositions relate *concepts* (theoretical), hypotheses relate
  *measures* (empirical). Use propositions when the purpose is to present a new theoretical
  position; they force concreteness and seed future tests. Don't use propositions to *summarize a
  literature*.

## 4. The diagnostic — how to use this in a session

When a user asks "is this theory?" / "why is my theory weak?":

1. **Scan their theory section against the five impostors.** Name which ones it leans on. Most
   "weak theory" sections are 80% references + variable lists + a figure + hypotheses, with the
   *Why* missing.
2. **Find the missing building block.** Almost always it is **Why** (no causal logic) or
   **When-Where-Who** (no boundary conditions, so the claim floats free of context).
3. **State the gap plainly and constructively**: e.g. *"You have a clear What and How — a model
   of constructs and arrows — but the Why is thin: the manuscript names cognitive-load theory but
   never spells out the mechanism by which load produces the effect. That mechanism is the
   contribution; right now it's implied, not argued."*
4. **Then move to building**: supply the Why via a reasoning mode (`reasoning-modes.md`), choose
   the right form (`forms-of-theorizing.md`), and make it land (`making-contributions-interesting.md`).

> Reframe weaknesses as opportunities. The fastest route from "weak theory" to "publishable" is
> usually not more data or more variables — it is making the *why* explicit, and bounding the
> claim with honest *when/where/who*.
