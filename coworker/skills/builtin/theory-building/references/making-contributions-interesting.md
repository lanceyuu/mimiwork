# Making a Contribution Interesting — and Positioning It

Sources:
- Davis (1971), *That's Interesting!*, Philosophy of the Social Sciences 1: 309–344.
- Locke & Golden-Biddle (1997), *Constructing Opportunities for Contribution*, AMJ 40: 1023–1062.
- Alvesson & Sandberg (2011), *Generating Research Questions through Problematization*, AMR 36:
  247–271.
- Ravasi, *Engaging with the Literature* (PhD seminar) — literature-review craft.

**The governing insight (Davis):** theories are noticed, cited, and remembered not because they
are *true* but because they are **interesting**. A claim is interesting when it **challenges a
taken-for-granted assumption** of its audience. Too little challenge → "obvious / so what?"; too
much → "absurd / I don't believe it." The contribution lives in the **sweet spot**: it denies a
weakly-held part of the audience's belief system while affirming a strongly-held part.

This is also a warning (SIOP debate; Pillutla & Thau): chasing "interesting" can degrade into
manufacturing counterintuitive "facts" and **HARKing** (hypothesizing after results are known).
Interestingness should *reveal a real tension*, never invent a fake one. Use the catalogue below
to *find* the genuine assumption your work overturns — not to dress up a thin result.

## 1. The interestingness sweet spot

To make a contribution land, finish this sentence for the target audience:

> "Past work assumes **X**; this paper shows that, in reality, **not-X** (under conditions C)."

- If you cannot name the **X** (the taken-for-granted belief you overturn), the contribution will
  read as obvious or as a mere gap-fill.
- If **not-X** denies something the audience holds *too* strongly, they reject it as absurd — so
  pair the denial with an affirmation of something they *do* hold, and supply the logic + boundary
  conditions.

Use the twelve types as a generator: run the user's finding through each lens and ask "does my
result flip a default belief of this kind?"

## 2. Davis's twelve types of interesting proposition

Each type has two symmetric forms ("what seems A is really B" / "what seems B is really A").
Examples are illustrative.

1. **Organization** — what seems *disorganized/unstructured* is really *organized/structured*
   (garbage-can model) — or what seems organized is really disorganized / organized differently
   (postmodern critiques of culture).
2. **Composition** — what seem *heterogeneous, assorted* phenomena are really a *single* element
   (transaction cost theory) — or a *single* phenomenon is really *assorted* elements
   (culture vs sub-cultures).
3. **Abstraction (whole vs part)** — what seems an *individual* phenomenon is really *holistic*
   (cultural theory) — or what seems holistic is really individual / particular (Hofstede).
4. **Generalization** — what seems a *local* phenomenon is really *general* (corporate
   entrepreneurship) — or what seems general is really *local* (cross-cultural studies).
5. **Stabilization** — what seems *stable/unchanging* is really *unstable/changing* (identity
   change) — or what seems changing is really *stable* (institutional theory).
6. **Function** — what seems to function *ineffectively* really functions *effectively* (strategic
   planning; brainstorming) — or what seems effective really functions *ineffectively* (rhetoric
   of TQM).
7. **Evaluation** — what seems a *bad* phenomenon is really *good* (lack of consensus aiding
   decisions) — or what seems good is really *bad* (critical analysis of culture).
8. **Co-relation** — what seem *unrelated/independent* phenomena are really *interdependent*
   (configuration theory) — or what seem related are really *unrelated* (strategy and survival in
   population ecology).
9. **Co-existence** — what seem phenomena that *can* coexist really *cannot* (legitimacy and
   efficiency) — or what seem phenomena that cannot coexist really *can* (identity as claims and
   beliefs; strong culture and change).
10. **Co-variation** — what seems a *positive* co-variation is really *negative* (change and
    survival in population ecology) — or a *negative* co-variation is really *positive* (control
    and productivity: Theory X vs Theory Y).
11. **Opposition** — what seem *similar/near-identical* phenomena are really *opposite* (types of
    sensemaking) — or what seem *opposite* are really *similar* (discourse theory and "text").
12. **Causation** — what seems the *independent* variable is really the *dependent* one
    ("strategy follows structure") — or what seems dependent is really *independent* (Weber:
    protestant ethic → capitalism).

## 3. Positioning — constructing an opportunity for contribution (Locke & Golden-Biddle, 1997)

"What counts as a contribution is that which is perceived as unique or novel **in light of the
extant literature**." A contribution is *constructed*, in two moves. Theory development is itself
an act of **creative sensemaking** — the interpretive frame, not the raw observations, is what
contributes (Astley).

### Move 1 — Construct intertextual coherence (build the "map" of the territory)
Show the reader a coherent body of work *so that* you can then show what's wrong with it:
- **Synthesized coherence** — "Hey, there's a territory!" Draw connections between studies *not
  usually cited together* (e.g. materiality + sensemaking) to constitute a new area.
- **Progressive coherence** — "the map changed over time." Show how a research program advanced
  (e.g. organizational identity moving from assumed-stable to contested).
- **Non-coherence** — "the maps disagree." Surface disagreement among works in a common program
  (e.g. culture as norms vs culture as toolkit).

### Move 2 — Problematize (show why the map is inadequate → your work is needed)
- **Incompleteness** — "some areas are uncharted." A gap needing further specification. **Weakest
  form** — pure gap-spotting "is not primarily driven by mere gap-spotting in literature reviews"
  and rarely yields strong contributions on its own.
- **Inadequacy** — "the map overlooks something important." A perspective the literature has
  neglected (e.g. emotions in institutional theory). Stronger.
- **Incommensurability** — "the map is wrong / heading the wrong way." Argue the literature's
  direction or core assumptions are mistaken. **Strongest** — and riskiest.

> Diplomatic rule (Ravasi): always do justice to past studies and frame weaknesses as
> "opportunities for further development." Reviewers are drawn from the very authors you critique.
> Critique the *ideas*, credit the *people*.

## 4. Problematizing assumptions (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011)

The deepest contributions come from identifying and challenging the **assumptions** underlying
existing literature — not from spotting gaps *within* its assumptions. Types of assumption to
target, from easiest to most ambitious:
- **In-house assumptions** — shared within a school of thought.
- **Root-metaphor / field assumptions** — broad images underlying a paradigm.
- **Paradigmatic / ideological / ontological assumptions** — the hardest to see and most powerful
  to challenge.

This is the "spotlight counterfactual" of `reasoning-modes.md` made into a method: ask *"what must
be true for this literature to make sense — and what if it isn't?"* Then — crucially — follow
through into a constructive alternative (otherwise it's a prod, not a contribution).

## 5. Literature-review craft (Ravasi)

A theory/literature section's job is **not to summarize** but to **add conceptual value** — to
help readers see a pattern they had not noticed.

1. **Be inclusive and comprehensive** — search adjacent domains (sociology, history, psychology,
   economics); other disciplines may know more than yours.
2. **Define scope carefully** — too broad → lost; too narrow → irrelevant.
3. **Read the whole paper** (the golden rule), not just abstracts — some papers can be
   re-interpreted through a different lens.
4. **Keep asking "what do we really know?"** — is the evidence convincing? are there forgotten
   boundary conditions? has the phenomenon changed?
5. **Reveal patterns, don't list** — what are studies trying to explain? what kinds of explanation
   do they offer? different levels of analysis? different ontologies? consistent terminology?
6. **Hunt for accepted gaps and contradictions** — what are the common assumptions; can they be
   challenged? do metaphors conceal a lack of theory development?
7. **Use a comparative table — not a summary table.** This is the highest-leverage artifact: it
   turns a list of study summaries into a *map of similarities and differences* across dimensions
   (central issue, assumptions, mechanism, level of analysis, contribution), and doubles as the
   **blueprint for the theory section**. Offer to build one for any user working on a theory or
   literature section. (Examples: retrospective vs prospective sensemaking; agency vs strategic-
   choice vs resource-dependence vs political views of boards.)

> When in doubt, the move that most reliably converts a literature review into a *contribution* is:
> build the comparative map → name the shared assumption → problematize it (inadequacy or
> incommensurability) → resolve the tension with a named reasoning move. That chain is the spine of
> a strong "front end."
