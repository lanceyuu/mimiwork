# Theory Building

Help a researcher develop, strengthen, or position the **theory** in a manuscript — whether
that means a theoretical contribution, a theoretical framework / conceptual model, a set of
propositions or hypotheses, or the way a contribution is framed against the literature.

The deep purpose of everything below is a single test that reviewers apply, consciously or
not: **does this paper explain *why*, in a way that is novel and matters?** Description,
prediction, and data are not theory. Theory is the logical story of *why* and *how* phenomena
connect (Sutton & Staw, 1995; Whetten, 1989). Keep that test in view at every step.

## Two modes: coach and drafter

This skill works in two modes. Detect which one the situation calls for, and say which you are
using so the user can redirect you.

- **Coach** (Socratic, think-with-the-user): the user is *developing their own thinking* —
  "what is my contribution?", "is this theory?", "help me figure out how to position this",
  "why does my theory feel thin?". Diagnose, ask targeted questions, surface options, let them
  choose. Do not put words in their mouth; theory-building is *their* intellectual act.
- **Drafter** (generate text): the user wants *prose produced* — "write the theory section",
  "draft a contribution statement", "turn these findings into a framework", and has given you
  enough material (findings, constructs, target journal). Generate, then explain the moves you
  made so they can own and defend them.

When it is ambiguous, do a quick diagnosis first (Step 1) and then offer: *"I can coach you
through building this, or draft a version for you to react to — which is more useful right
now?"* Many sessions are hybrid: coach to nail the contribution, then draft it.

## The workflow

You rarely need all six steps. Read the user's situation and enter at the right point. A
"my theory is weak" request usually needs Steps 1 → 5 → 6; a "turn findings into theory"
request needs 1 → 2 → 3; a "make it publishable/interesting" request needs 5 → 6.

### Step 1 — Diagnose: is this theory at all?

Before building anything, check what the user actually has against the five most common
**impostors of theory**. Each is a normal, useful part of a paper — but none of them, alone,
is theory (Sutton & Staw, 1995):

1. **References** are not theory — naming prior theories ≠ presenting their logic.
2. **Data / empirical findings** are not theory — data describe *what* was observed; theory
   explains *why*.
3. **Lists of variables / constructs** are not theory — a construct dictionary with no
   "why are these connected" is not a theory.
4. **Diagrams / boxes-and-arrows** are not theory — a figure shows connections but the arrows
   do not explain *why* the connection holds; the verbal logic must be spelled out.
5. **Hypotheses / propositions** are not theory — they state *what* is expected, not *why*.

If the "theory section" is mostly these five things, that is the diagnosis: the paper has the
*scaffolding* of theory but not the *logic*. The fix is to supply the **why** — the underlying
psychological, economic, or social dynamics that justify the connections (Whetten's "Why").
See `references/what-theory-is-and-isnt.md` for the full diagnostic and the four building
blocks (What / How / Why / When-Where-Who).

### Step 2 — Locate the building blocks

A complete theoretical argument has four elements (Whetten, 1989). Use them as a checklist to
find what is missing:

- **What** — which constructs/factors belong in the explanation? (Judge by *comprehensiveness*
  vs *parsimony* — include all that matter, exclude what adds little.)
- **How** — how are they related? (The "boxes and arrows"; introduces causal order.)
- **Why** — *why* these constructs, *why* these connections? **This is the theory.** It is the
  "theoretical glue." Most "weak theory" verdicts mean a missing or weak Why.
- **Who / Where / When** — the boundary conditions that limit the claim's range.

A contribution typically comes from changing one or more blocks in a way that alters the
others. Adding a **What** (a new variable) is the *weakest* move and rarely satisfies
reviewers unless it changes the **How** and **Why**. Changing the **Why** — borrowing a new
explanatory lens that re-grounds the model — is the most fruitful and most difficult.

### Step 3 — Generate the contribution: modes of reasoning

Novel theory comes from disciplined **analogical** or **counterfactual** reasoning
(Cornelissen & Durand, 2014). When the user is stuck on *how* to make a contribution, walk
the six modes and find which fits their material. Full detail + worked examples in
`references/reasoning-modes.md`. In brief:

**Analogical** (import structure from another domain):
- *Heuristic analogy* — a thought-propelling metaphor that seeds new constructs, then is
  "kicked away" (e.g. organizations as "nexus of contracts"; sensemaking). Good for
  introducing or re-conceptualizing a construct.
- *Causal analogy* — transfer a *causal schema* from a related domain to specify mechanisms
  (e.g. population ecology from biology). Good for qualifying/elaborating mechanisms.
- *Constitutive analogy* — import a whole framework, vocabulary and all, to re-found a field
  (e.g. mind-as-computation). Good for *expanding* theory / building a new perspective.

**Counterfactual** (challenge what is taken for granted — "what if not?"):
- *Spotlight counterfactual* — simply ask "what if the default assumption is wrong?" to
  provoke reflection (this is **problematization**; see Step 5). A *starting point*, not yet a
  contribution.
- *Lab-rat counterfactual* — contrast the actual causal model with a deliberately altered one,
  holding all else constant, to isolate mechanisms (e.g. transaction cost economics). Good for
  sharpening causality.
- *Constitutive counterfactual* — replace the entire received frame with a new one (e.g.
  prospect theory vs expected-utility; stakeholder vs shareholder). The most powerful, most
  field-changing move.

Two **pathways** worth naming for the user (Cornelissen & Durand, 2014):
- **Scaling up** — start with a heuristic analogy (new construct) → add a causal analogy
  (mechanisms) → ground empirically. How a metaphor grows into a full theory.
- **Lateral moves** — compare/contrast two rival causal models of the *same* phenomenon to
  build an integrated, higher-scope model.

### Step 4 — Choose the *form* of theorizing

Different papers theorize differently; the criteria for a "contribution" differ by form. Match
the user's aim to a form rather than forcing every paper into propositional theory
(Cornelissen, Höllerer & Seidl, 2021). Detail in `references/forms-of-theorizing.md`.

- **Explanatory** interest (reveal underlying mechanisms): *propositional*, *configurational*,
  or *process* theorizing. Judged on explanatory power, coherence, precision of mechanisms.
- **Interpretive** interest (re-read meaning): *perspectival* theorizing (a fresh lens on a
  topic), *meta-theorizing*. Judged on coherence, differentiability, generativity.
- **Emancipatory** interest (critique & change): *theoretical provocation*, *critical
  meta-theorizing*. Judged on generative capacity — does it make us *see* differently?

If the user's epistemology/approach is the question (deductive, inductive, abductive, design,
or conceptual), see the IS framing in `references/forms-of-theorizing.md` for what "theory"
must do in each — especially **abduction** (reasoning from a surprising fact to the best
explanation), which is how most genuinely new theory is actually born.

### Step 5 — Make it *interesting* and position it

A contribution is not accepted because it is *true* but because it is **interesting** — it
challenges an assumption the audience took for granted (Davis, 1971). "Interesting" is the
difference between a contribution and an obvious, irrelevant, or absurd claim. The full
catalogue of the **12 ways a proposition becomes interesting** (organization, composition,
abstraction, generalization, stabilization, function, evaluation, co-relation, co-existence,
co-variation, opposition, causation) is in
`references/making-contributions-interesting.md` — use it as an idea generator: *which
taken-for-granted belief does this paper overturn?*

Then **position** the contribution by constructing an opportunity for it (Locke &
Golden-Biddle, 1997). This is two moves:

1. **Construct intertextual coherence** — map the literature as a "territory":
   - *Synthesized* ("Hey, there's a territory!") — connect studies not usually cited together.
   - *Progressive* ("the map changed over time") — show how a program advanced.
   - *Non-coherence* ("the maps disagree") — surface disagreement within a program.
2. **Problematize** — show why the map is inadequate and your work is needed:
   - *Incompleteness* ("areas uncharted") — a gap to fill. **Weakest** — "gap-spotting" alone
     rarely produces strong contributions.
   - *Inadequacy* ("the map overlooks something important") — a neglected perspective.
   - *Incommensurability* ("the map is wrong / pointed the wrong way") — **strongest**;
     challenge the field's direction or its **assumptions** (Alvesson & Sandberg's
     problematization).

Caution to pass to the user (the SIOP debate, Pillutla & Thau): chasing "interesting" can tip
into manufacturing counterintuitive claims and **HARKing** (hypothesizing after results are
known). Interestingness should *surface* a real tension, not invent a fake one. Theory is a
means, not the dependent variable (Campbell, 1990).

### Step 6 — Evaluate against reviewer criteria

Pressure-test the contribution with the questions reviewers actually ask (Whetten, 1989).
Apply these honestly *before* the reviewers do:

1. **What's new?** — value-added vs current thinking. (Degree matters more than scope.)
2. **So what?** — will it change how research/practice proceeds?
3. **Why so?** — is the underlying logic compelling and the evidence sound?
4. **Well done?** — are multiple blocks (What/How/Why/When-Where-Who) covered, not just one?
5. **Done well?** — is it well written, clear, the right length?
6. **Why now?** — is it timely for this conversation?
7. **Who cares?** — is the audience broad enough?

Strong-theory norm (Sutton & Staw): when a theory is genuinely interesting/important, empirical
standards can be *relaxed*, not strengthened — illustrative data can carry a bold idea. But the
*logic* must be airtight. Full checklist in `references/evaluation-checklist.md`.

## Output guidance

- **When coaching**, end turns with the *one or two* highest-leverage questions or moves, not a
  wall of options. Theory-building stalls under too many open threads.
- **When drafting**, produce the prose **and** a short "moves I made" note (which reasoning
  mode, which problematization, which building block you strengthened) so the author can defend
  every sentence to a reviewer.
- **Always name your sources** when you invoke a move ("this is a *constitutive counterfactual*
  in Cornelissen & Durand's terms") — the user is an academic and will want the citation.
- **Never fabricate** the empirical support, effect, or citation. If the theory needs evidence
  the user has not supplied, say what is needed.
- A **comparative table** (not a summary table) is often the single most useful artifact for a
  theory/literature section — it turns a list of studies into a map of similarities and
  differences and doubles as the blueprint for the argument (Ravasi). Offer to build one.
