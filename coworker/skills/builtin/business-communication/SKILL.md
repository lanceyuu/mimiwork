# Business Communication

Business readers don't read — they triage. Every message competes with forty others for
thirty seconds of attention. The craft is front-loading: the reader who stops after the first
two lines should still know what you need from them.

## The universal skeleton (BLUF)

**Bottom Line Up Front**, always:

1. **The ask or the news, in line one.** "Recommend we delay the launch to March 4 — decision
   needed by Friday." Context comes after, never before.
2. **Why, in 2–3 bullets.** The minimum reasoning that makes the ask credible.
3. **What happens next.** Who does what by when. A message with no owner and no date is FYI,
   and should say so.

Subject lines carry the verb: "Decision needed: launch date" beats "Launch update".

## Calibrate to the reader

- **Executives**: conclusion + options + your recommendation. One screen. They pay for
  judgment, not raw analysis — attach the analysis, don't inline it. Anticipate the two
  questions they'll ask and answer them preemptively.
- **Peers**: context can be leaner (shared ground), asks must be exact ("review §2 for the
  pricing logic by Thu" — never "thoughts?").
- **Clients / external**: warmer opening, zero internal jargon, and never expose internal
  disagreement or process. Commitments only in writing you can keep.
- **Cross-cultural / non-native readers**: shorter sentences, no idioms, dates as 4 March
  (not 3/4), explicit rather than implied asks.

## Difficult messages

**Saying no**: acknowledge the request's legitimacy → the no, clearly, without a fake maybe →
the reason (one, honest) → an alternative if a real one exists. Burying the no in cushioning
reads as a yes.

**Bad news**: deliver it in the first sentence — delay reads as concealment. Then: what
happened, impact, what you're doing about it, when they'll hear next. Own it without
groveling; one apology, not three.

**Escalation**: facts before feelings. What was expected, what happened, impact in business
terms, what you've already tried, the specific decision or intervention you need. Copy people
for a reason you could state out loud.

**Disagreement upward**: state you'll commit to whatever is decided, then make the strongest
version of your case once, in writing, with the trade-off framed in THEIR objectives.

## Formats

**Status update**: traffic-light headline (on track / at risk / blocked) → 3 bullets of
substance → risks with owners → asks. Same structure every time; readers navigate by habit.

**Executive summary**: it is not an introduction — it IS the document for 80% of readers.
Findings and recommendation with the key numbers, standing fully alone.

**Meeting follow-up**: decisions made, actions with owner + date, open questions with owner.
Send within 24 hours; the writer of the recap owns the record.

**Announcement**: what's changing, when, why (brief, honest), what it means for the reader,
where to ask questions. The "what it means for you" section is the whole message — write it
per audience if impacts differ.

## Tone rules

- Confident without hedging ("I think maybe we could possibly…" → "I recommend").
- Warm without padding — one line of human is plenty; three is throat-clearing.
- Never write angry: name the frustration to yourself, then write the version that gets the
  outcome. Sarcasm has negative ROI in writing, always.
- Praise in public specifics, criticize in private specifics.

When drafting for a user, ask (or infer from context) the three calibration facts if missing:
who reads it, what they must do after reading, and what the reader already knows. Offer ONE
draft plus a one-line note on the main judgment call you made — not three drafts to choose from.
