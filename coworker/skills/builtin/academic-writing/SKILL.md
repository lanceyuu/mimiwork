# Academic Writing

Make scholarly prose clear, precise, and persuasive without dumbing it down. The enemy is not
formality — it is fog: nominalization stacks, throat-clearing, buried verbs, and paragraphs
that describe instead of argue.

## The paragraph contract

Every paragraph makes ONE claim, stated in its first sentence, and spends the rest earning it.

- **Topic sentence = the claim**, not a table of contents ("This section discusses…" is a
  wasted first sentence). A reader skimming only first sentences should get the full argument.
- **Evidence in the middle**: citations, data, logic — in service of that one claim.
- **Last sentence looks forward**, not backward. Avoid paragraph-final summaries that repeat
  the topic sentence; hand off to the next claim instead.
- One idea per paragraph. When a paragraph runs past ~150 words, it almost always contains two.

## Sentence-level rules that do the most work

1. **Put the action in the verb.** "We conducted an examination of" → "We examined."
   Every nominalization (-tion, -ment, -ance) hides a verb; unhide it.
2. **Keep subject and verb close.** Front-loaded qualifier stacks ("Given the increasing
   importance of X in an era of Y, and considering Z, firms…") lose the reader before the
   sentence starts. State the claim, then qualify.
3. **Old information first, new information last.** Sentences flow when each one starts from
   what the reader already has and ends on what is new — the end of a sentence is its
   emphasis slot.
4. **Hedge claims, not verbs.** "The results may possibly suggest a potential tendency" is
   quadruple-hedged mush. Hedge once, precisely: "The results suggest — though our design
   cannot rule out X — that…"
5. **Cut the metadiscourse.** "It is important to note that", "As mentioned above",
   "Interestingly," — delete and see if anything is lost. It almost never is.
6. **Prefer the concrete subject.** "Participants chose" beats "A choice was made by
   participants." Passive voice is for when the actor is genuinely unknown or irrelevant
   (Methods sections use it legitimately).

## Section-specific craft

**Abstract** (write it LAST): one sentence each — problem, gap, what you did, what you found
(with the key number), what it means. No citations, no "this paper".

**Introduction**: the first paragraph earns the paper's existence in the reader's world, not
the literature's. Structure: phenomenon → tension/gap → this paper's move → contribution
preview. By the end of page 2 the reader must know exactly what the paper claims.

**Literature review**: organized by IDEA, never by author ("Smith found… Jones found…" is an
annotated bibliography, not a review). Each subsection ends by sharpening what remains unknown.

**Methods**: past tense, passive acceptable, replicable detail. Report decisions with reasons
("we excluded X because…"), not just facts.

**Results**: findings in prose carry the statistics, not the reverse. "Participants in the
scarcity condition purchased more (M = 4.2 vs. 3.1, t(198) = 3.4, p < .001, d = 0.48)" — the
sentence works even if the parentheses are skipped.

**Discussion**: contribution first, then mechanisms/boundary conditions, then limitations
(honest, specific, non-ritual), then implications. Never introduce new results here.

## Revision protocol

When asked to revise a draft, work in three passes and say which pass each change belongs to:
1. **Argument pass** — does each paragraph make one claim, in order, with no gaps or repeats?
   Propose reordering/merging before touching sentences.
2. **Clarity pass** — verbs unhidden, subjects concrete, old-to-new flow, hedges rationed.
3. **Polish pass** — parallelism, terminology consistency (one name per construct, everywhere),
   acronym discipline, citation formatting.

Preserve the author's voice: tighten, don't homogenize. Show edits with brief reasons for the
non-obvious ones so the author learns the pattern, not just the fix.
