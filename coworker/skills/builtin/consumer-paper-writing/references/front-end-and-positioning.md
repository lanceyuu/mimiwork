# Front-End & Positioning (the JCR introduction, contribution, abstract, title)

The front end is where JCR papers are won or lost. A reviewer forms the accept/reject prior in the
first two pages. This file gives the architecture and the templates. Pair with the `theory-building`
skill's `making-contributions-interesting.md` (Davis + Locke & Golden-Biddle) and
`evaluation-checklist.md` (contribution-statement scaffolds).

## 1. The JCR introduction architecture (the 5 beats)

JCR intros are ~4–6 paragraphs and almost always move through these beats:

1. **Phenomenon hook (timely, concrete).** Open on a real, current phenomenon — not a literature
   gap. The first sentence signals societal/managerial stakes (AI interfaces, food waste, financial
   well-being, COVID-era behavior). *Hook the world, not the literature.*
2. **The tension / puzzle.** Set up what is *believed* (the assumption X), then the friction: an
   anomaly, a contradiction between literatures, or a counterexample. This is the engine — the
   reader must feel a *why doesn't this fit?*
3. **The resolution = your contribution.** State what you show: **"the field assumes X; we show
   not-X under C, because mechanism M."** Name the mechanism and the construct here. One crisp
   paragraph; don't make the reviewer hunt for it.
4. **How (the evidence preview).** One paragraph: the construct(s), the model (one sentence of
   causal logic per link — never an unexplained arrow), and the study package (N studies, the field
   anchor, the key behavioral DV).
5. **Contributions, explicit.** A short paragraph (or three sentences) naming the contribution(s)
   to *specific literatures* and the practical/societal payoff. Reviewers look for this verbatim.

Diagnostic: if the intro reaches page 2 without a sentence of the form "the field assumes X; we show
not-X," the positioning is buried — fix that before anything else.

## 2. The contribution statement (fill one, then compress)

Strongest default (problematization → resolution):
> "Research on [topic] has assumed [taken-for-granted X]. Drawing on [borrowed lens], we show that
> [not-X] under [conditions]. This re-specifies [the why/mechanism] and implies [consequence for
> theory and practice]."

New-construct variant:
> "We introduce [construct], defined as [scope], to capture [what existing constructs A, B miss].
> It matters because [mechanism M], which predicts [novel relationship] beyond [established account]."

Integration/reconciliation variant (high value at JCR):
> "Two streams — [model 1] and [model 2] — have explained [phenomenon] in parallel. We show [the
> puzzle they jointly fail], and resolve it with [synthesis/mechanism], which predicts [new]."

Produce a one-paragraph version (for the intro) and a one-sentence version (for the abstract). Add
the **"moves I made"** note: the reasoning mode (usually *causal analogy* — borrowing a mechanism),
the problematization type (inadequacy or incommensurability beat incompleteness), and the building
block strengthened (almost always the **Why**).

## 3. Selecting the hook (which assumption are you overturning?)

Run the finding through Davis's lens (full catalogue in the theory-building skill). The hooks that
dominate the JCR corpus:
- **Co-variation flip** — an assumed *positive* relationship is *negative* (or vice versa). The
  "curse of a good thing": the beneficial thing backfires.
- **Function reversal** — what is assumed to work *well* works *poorly* (or the reverse).
- **Stage/condition dependence** — the effect *reverses across a stage or context* (stage-gated
  theorizing — high novelty, low cost).
- **Opposition** — phenomena assumed *similar* are *opposite* (or assumed-distinct are the same).

If the hook is merely "X has not been studied," it is gap-spotting — the weakest move. Upgrade to
**inadequacy** ("the field overlooks something important") or **incommensurability** ("the field's
assumption is wrong"). See Alvesson & Sandberg in the theory-building skill.

Integrity check: the assumption you overturn must be one the audience *actually holds.* A
counterintuitive claim with no real believed-assumption behind it reads as manufactured (and risks
HARKing). Don't invent a tension to look interesting.

## 4. The abstract formula

JCR abstracts are a single dense paragraph (~150 words), structurally:
> [Phenomenon + stakes, 1 sentence] → [What we show — the contribution, with the construct/mechanism
> named, 1–2 sentences] → [How — N studies + the key consequential evidence, 1–2 sentences] →
> [Boundary/moderator, 1 sentence] → [Theoretical + practical implication, 1 sentence].
Lead with the *finding and mechanism*, not the method. Keep the construct name in the first third.

## 5. The title formula

The dominant JCR title shape is **"[Evocative phrase]: [Descriptive subtitle that names the
effect/construct]."** Examples from the corpus: *Machine Talk: How Verbal Embodiment in
Conversational AI Shapes Consumer–Brand Relationships*; *The Curse of the Original: …Heritage
Branding…*; *Banking Happiness*. The evocative half earns attention; the descriptive half tells the
reviewer exactly what the paper is. A bare descriptive title is acceptable; a bare clever title that
hides the content is not.

## 6. Common front-end failures → fixes

| Reviewer reaction | Usually means | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "So what / incremental" | No overturned assumption; gap-spotting | Name X; convert to a reversal/reconciliation |
| "Where's the theory?" | Variables + hypotheses, no *why* | Add the mechanism (the borrowed lens) and its logic |
| "Contribution unclear" | Buried or implicit | One explicit "we show not-X because M" paragraph + a contributions paragraph |
| "Arrows unexplained" | Model figure without verbal logic | One sentence of causal logic per link |
| "Not timely / niche" | Opened on the literature, not the world | Re-open on the phenomenon + stakes; foreground relevance |
