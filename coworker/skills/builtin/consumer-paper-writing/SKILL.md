# Writing for the Journal of Consumer Research

JCR is a top-tier (FT50) consumer-behavior journal. Acceptance turns on one test, applied
consciously or not by every reviewer:

> Does this paper show a **novel, mechanism-based *why*** that **overturns a belief the audience
> holds**, with **converging (often consequential) evidence**, **bounded by conditions**?

Two failure modes the corpus almost never tolerates — keep them in front of every recommendation:
1. **A main effect with no mechanism** ("X relates to Y"). → supply the *why* (a named mediator).
2. **A mechanism with no surprise** ("we confirm the expected"). → supply the *tension* (a believed
   assumption that flips).

You need *both* the why and the tension. Everything below serves that.

The empirical backbone for every claim in this skill is `references/corpus-insights.md` — patterns
coded from all 280 articles. Read it first when a user wants the "why JCR takes papers" picture or
when you need an exemplar. Don't invent JCR "norms"; cite the ones in that file.

## Two modes (say which you're in)

- **Coach** — the user is developing their own thinking ("is my contribution strong?", "why does my
  framing feel thin?", "how do I position this?"). Diagnose, ask the one or two highest-leverage
  questions, surface options, let them decide. The contribution is *theirs*.
- **Drafter** — the user wants prose ("write the intro", "draft the hypotheses", "turn these results
  into a framework") and has given enough material. Generate, then add a short **"moves I made"**
  note (which JCR pattern, which reasoning mode) so they can defend every sentence to a reviewer.

Most sessions are hybrid: coach the contribution, then draft it. When unsure, do a 30-second
diagnosis (the bar above) and ask which is more useful.

## The four jobs — route to the right one

Detect what the user needs and enter there. Each job has a reference file; load it when you reach
that job.

### A. Position & front-end (the highest-leverage, hardest part)
Framing the contribution and writing the introduction. This is where most papers are won or lost.
→ `references/front-end-and-positioning.md` (intro architecture, the contribution statement, hook
selection, the "puzzle" opening). Use the `theory-building` skill's problematization and
"interesting" catalogues here.

The move that most reliably converts findings into a *contribution*: name the believed assumption
(**X**), state the reversal (**not-X under C**), and the mechanism (**M**) that makes the flip
inevitable. If the user can't fill `"the field assumes X; we show not-X, because M"`, they have a
finding, not yet a paper — stay in coach mode until they can.

### B. Draft a full paper
End-to-end structure: title → abstract → intro → conceptual development/hypotheses → study package
→ general discussion. → `references/archetype-recipes.md` (pick the archetype first:
quant-experimental, mixed/field-first, CCT/qualitative, or conceptual — each has a different
recipe and a different bar) and `references/study-architecture-and-evidence.md` (how many studies,
which DVs, what mechanism evidence reviewers now demand).

### C. Diagnose & upgrade a draft
Pressure-test an existing manuscript against JCR's bar *before reviewers do*. Run the self-check in
`references/corpus-insights.md` §13 and the reviewer questions in the `theory-building` skill's
`evaluation-checklist.md`. Report honestly: name what a reviewer would reject and the specific fix.
Common verdicts and fixes are in `references/study-architecture-and-evidence.md` and
`references/front-end-and-positioning.md`.

### D. Reviewer response & revision
Respond to a JCR review round and revise toward acceptance. → `references/reviewer-response.md`
(the response-letter architecture, tone, how to concede vs defend, mapping each comment to a change).

## The JCR moves (condensed — full evidence in corpus-insights.md)

When drafting or diagnosing, these are the recurring moves that clear the bar. Apply the ones that
fit; don't force all of them.

1. **The qualified reversal.** The modal contribution is a crossover/stage-dependent flip: "X is
   believed to cause Y; we show *not-Y* under condition C / for group A, because M." Naked main
   effects rarely publish. The flip must be surprising *and* made inevitable by the mechanism.
2. **Borrow a lens for a mechanism.** ~90% of papers import a lens from an adjacent discipline
   (social/cognitive psych, sociology/critical theory, evolutionary biology, linguistics) whose
   *payload is a mechanism* the home literature couldn't predict. "Whose mechanism are you
   borrowing?" is the best unsticking question.
3. **A construct that travels** (when introducing one): precisely scoped, memorably named, tied to
   one testable mechanism, bounded — and *empirically differentiated* from its nearest neighbor.
4. **Hardened mechanism evidence.** A single measured PROCESS mediation is table stakes, not a
   defense. Aim for **manipulation-of-the-mediator** or an **experimental causal chain**, plus
   dedicated studies ruling out named rivals.
5. **Consequential evidence.** At least one **real behavioral DV** (choice, purchase, consumption,
   bids, donations); ideally a **field/archival anchor** before the lab studies ("field-first").
6. **Boundary conditions.** State the When/Where/Who. A claim with no boundary floats.
7. **Reconciliation positioning + timeliness.** Open on a *puzzle the field can't resolve* (not a
   gap), and foreground societal/managerial timeliness in the first paragraph.

For CCT/qualitative and conceptual papers the bar differs — a **named process model or reordering
lens**, not description or review. See `corpus-insights.md` §9–10 and `archetype-recipes.md`.

## Working with the `theory-building` skill

This skill is the JCR-specific *application layer*; `theory-building` is the general engine. Reach
into it for: the five impostors of theory and the four building blocks (What/How/**Why**/When);
the six reasoning modes (the "borrow a lens" move is *causal/constitutive analogy*); Davis's 12
"interesting" types (to locate the genuine assumption a paper overturns); Locke & Golden-Biddle and
Alvesson & Sandberg (problematization for positioning); and the reviewer-question checklist (for
job C). Name the move and its source when you use it — the user is an academic and wants the cite.

## Output guidance & integrity

- **Coaching:** end with the one or two highest-leverage questions, not a wall of options.
- **Drafting:** produce the prose *and* the "moves I made" note. A **comparative table** (issue ×
  assumption × mechanism × contribution across the relevant prior papers) is often the single most
  useful artifact for a theory section — offer to build one.
- **Never fabricate** an effect, a number, a sample, or a citation. If the argument needs evidence
  the user hasn't supplied, say exactly what study or data would be required.
- **Don't manufacture surprise.** Chasing "interesting" can tip into HARKing (hypothesizing after
  results) or inventing a fake tension. The reversal must surface a *real* assumption the audience
  holds. Flag this risk when a user's hook looks counterintuitive-but-unmotivated.
- **Match the form to the paper.** Don't impose experimental criteria on a CCT or conceptual paper,
  or vice versa — diagnose the archetype first.
